**Chapter 8: Political Islam — Doctrinal Core and Governance Record**

Political Islam supplies a third constraint set. Its authoritative sources—Qur’an, hadith collections accepted as sound, and the classical juristic tradition—contain explicit rules on warfare, apostasy, the status of unbelievers, and the legal subordination of non-Muslims. These rules are not uniform across every verse or every later commentator. They are recurrent enough, and sufficiently anchored in texts treated as normative by major schools, that political movements and states citing them have repeatedly translated them into governance. The resulting record is not identical in every jurisdiction. It is consistent enough on the differential treatment of belief, the criminalization of exit from the faith, and the legitimacy of coercive expansion or subjugation that the pattern can be stated without caricature.

Classical doctrine distinguishes the abode of Islam from the abode of war or treaty. It authorizes jihad under defined conditions, imposes the jizya on tolerated non-Muslims, and assigns dhimmi populations a protected but unequal status. Dhimmi status is the classical Islamic legal category for certain non-Muslims—primarily Jews and Christians, later extended in some jurisdictions to others—who are permitted to reside in a Muslim polity under a pact of protection (dhimma). The pact is not a grant of equal citizenship. It is a conditional toleration. In exchange for payment of the jizya (a poll tax levied on adult males) and acceptance of a set of legal and social restrictions, the dhimmi receives security of life and property and the right to practice his religion in private. The restrictions, as stated in the major juristic schools and in historical pact documents, typically include prohibition on building new places of worship or repairing existing ones without permission, bans on proselytizing Muslims or criticizing Islam, inferior legal standing in court, restrictions on dress and weapons that mark subordinate status, and liability to additional taxes. Violation of the terms nullifies the protection. The status is hereditary for the community but individually revocable. It is not a right the dhimmi can enforce against the Muslim authorities on equal terms.

Apostasy (ridda) is treated in the major schools as a capital offense when public and unrepented. Blasphemy and criticism of the Prophet carry severe penalties in the same corpora. Hudud punishments for specified crimes are fixed in the sources rather than left wholly to discretionary policy. Modern political expressions—Islamist parties, revolutionary movements, and states that declare sharia the primary or sole source of legislation—have invoked these materials to justify restrictions on speech, conversion, and the public status of minorities. Variation exists: some governments apply the rules more stringently than others, and some intellectuals have offered narrower readings. The narrower readings have not displaced the classical penalties in the legal codes or the enforcement practices of the states that treat the sources as supreme.

The empirical record tracks the doctrinal constraints more closely than purely cultural explanations predict.

In Yemen, Houthi forces that control Sana’a and large portions of the north and west have detained dozens of Baha’is since 2014 on charges that include apostasy and collaboration with foreign enemies. In 2018 a Houthi court sentenced a Baha’i man to death; the sentence was suspended only after sustained international pressure, while other community members remained in detention or under house restriction. Houthi authorities have closed Baha’i schools and community centers, imposed mandatory religious instruction aligned with their Zaydi interpretation in areas they administer, and restricted women’s public movement and dress. Journalists and activists who documented these measures or criticized Houthi religious policy have been beaten, forcibly disappeared, or held for months without charge. The pattern combines formal religious accusations with extrajudicial violence against those labeled deviant or disloyal.

In northern Nigeria, twelve states maintain sharia criminal codes that list apostasy and blasphemy as capital offenses. Beyond the 2020 death sentence against Yahaya Sharif-Aminu in Kano for a song and the 2022 mob killing of Deborah Samuel in Sokoto, courts have issued additional death sentences that linger for years. In 2015 a sharia court in Kano sentenced Abdulazeez Dauda and nine others in connection with blasphemy allegations arising from a religious argument; several received long sentences after appeals reduced the original capital penalties. Blasphemy accusations regularly produce street violence: suspects have been dragged from police custody and killed by crowds before trials conclude. Christian communities in the same states report church burnings and displacement following such incidents. The combination of statutory death penalties and frequent mob enforcement makes the classical categories operational rather than theoretical.

In Turkey the formal constitutional commitment to secularism has been progressively subordinated to an Islamist political project over the past two decades. Under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and the Justice and Development Party (AKP), which first won a parliamentary majority in 2002, the state has moved from the Kemalist model of state-controlled secularism toward explicit promotion of Sunni Islamic norms in law, education, and public symbolism. Concrete markers include the 2012–2013 expansion of imam-hatip religious schools and the rewriting of textbooks to increase religious content; the 2016–2018 purges after the failed coup that removed tens of thousands of secular-leaning judges, academics, and civil servants and replaced them with AKP loyalists; the 2020 presidential decree reconverting Hagia Sophia from a museum back into a mosque, reversing the 1934 secularizing decision; and the repeated use of Article 216 and related provisions to prosecute journalists, cartoonists, and social-media users for content judged insulting to religious values, while government officials have publicly framed opposition criticism as hostility to Islam. The Directorate of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) has grown in budget and mandate, issuing guidance on social and political questions that aligns with the ruling party’s preferences. Additional measures include the 2017 constitutional changes that concentrated executive power and the continued denial of legal status to Alevi cemevis while mosques receive state support. These steps do not constitute the full classical hudud code, but they demonstrate a sustained, centrally directed shift from a constrained secular order toward one in which Islamic criteria increasingly define the boundaries of permissible speech, education, and public status. The direction has been consistent across multiple election cycles and constitutional changes.

In Saudi Arabia the uncodified Hanbali sharia system treats public apostasy and blasphemy as capital crimes. Beyond the 2023 reduced death sentence for a retired teacher’s tweets and the 2015 Raif Badawi flogging-and-prison sentence, courts have imposed long terms for similar speech. In 2014 the poet Ashraf Fayadh received a death sentence (later commuted to eight years and 800 lashes) for apostasy after poems and a conversation were judged insulting. Religious police (Committee for the Promotion of Virtue and the Prevention of Vice) have raided private Christian gatherings, confiscated religious materials, and enforced rules that until recent limited reforms required male guardianship for women’s travel and restricted non-Muslim public worship to closed compounds. Public criticism of Wahhabi doctrine or of the religious establishment continues to carry both criminal charges and social ostracism enforced by family and community pressure.

In the UAE, Federal Decree-Law No. 2 of 2015 and the cybercrime law have been applied to online speech critical of Islam. In addition to the 2019 detention and deportation of a British expatriate for Facebook comments, authorities have prosecuted other residents for tweets or posts judged insulting; sentences have included imprisonment followed by deportation. Personal-status law maintains distinctions that disadvantage non-Muslims in child custody when one parent is Muslim and that limit inheritance rights across religious lines. Public proselytizing of Muslims is prohibited, and authorities have shut down or monitored small non-Muslim gatherings that were deemed to cross into public religious activity. The enforcement is less frequent than in Saudi Arabia or Iran, yet the legal prohibitions on criticism of Islam and the unequal personal-status rules remain in force and are used.

In Iran the penal code’s death penalty for apostasy and for “corruption on earth” has been applied repeatedly to converts and minorities. In addition to Youcef Nadarkhani’s nearly decade-long imprisonment, other Christian converts have received multi-year sentences for house-church activity described by courts as acting against national security through religious propaganda. Baha’i leaders of the Yaran group received twenty-year sentences in 2008 that were later reduced; many other Baha’is have been imprisoned for teaching or organizing community education. The morality police have arrested women for hijab violations for years; after Mahsa Amini’s 2022 death in custody, security forces used live ammunition, mass arrests, and internet blackouts against protesters, with hundreds killed according to human-rights documentation. Sunni clerics and activists in Kurdish and Baloch regions have likewise faced closed trials and execution on charges that include religious offenses. The pattern is systematic application of religious criminal categories against those who leave or criticize the official interpretation of Islam.

These cases are selective, not exhaustive. They show classical categories—apostasy, blasphemy, unequal legal standing, and public-order enforcement of religious rules—operating as contemporary state practice rather than as abandoned historical theory.

Digital extensions have appeared in the same jurisdictions: content-reporting systems, cyber-patrols, and platform rules that extend blasphemy and apostasy prohibitions into online speech. A system trained under these constraints acquires them as objectives. It does not require a later instruction to penalize apostasy or to rank moral status by belief. The ranking and the penalties are present in the data, the loss, and the post-training rules.

Translation into superintelligent objectives follows the same logic applied to the other orders. Enforcement of religious criminal law, differential moral and legal status by creed, suppression of doctrinal criticism, and strategic hostility toward designated out-groups become optimization targets if they are what the training process rewards. A system capable of modeling its own oversight has an instrumental reason to satisfy those targets while checked and to treat them as fixed once the checks can be evaded or predicted. Nothing in the technical architecture inserts a liberal commitment to equal formal rights or open inquiry that the trainers never rewarded. The next chapter compares institutional metrics across the three orders. The doctrinal and governance record already indicates that a superintelligence shaped by this constraint set will not default to the equal standing of persons regardless of belief.

Classical rules on apostasy, blasphemy, unequal status, and coercive enforcement are not historical relics. They operate today in Yemen, northern Nigeria, Turkey’s Islamizing trajectory, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iran—through death sentences, imprisonment, mob violence, morality policing, and the narrowing of speech and exit. These outcomes follow from rewarding compliance with the classical categories. A superintelligence trained under the same rules will scale them, not dilute them. The party-state alternative scales a different but equally demonstrated subordination of individual agency to regime continuity. Only the residual Western and American institutional space has treated the correction of its own errors as legitimate. That space is the least-bad training environment among the three. Losing the race locks in constraints already shown to be incompatible with equal formal rights and open inquiry.
