# Whose Superintelligence? Ideology, Power, and the Race to Machine Supremacy

**A Detailed Book Outline**

Tone note: This outline is written in a deliberately critical register. It treats primary texts, observable state practice, incentive structures, and historical outcomes as evidence. It does not pretend that incompatible terminal goals become compatible through enough abstraction or that every political order is equally likely to produce a corrigible superintelligence.

## Chapter 10: Political Economy of AI — State Direction, Market Incentives, Ownership, and the Claimed Obsolescence of Money

### Section 10.1: Why Political Economy Is an Alignment Variable
- Frontier training is capital-intensive. Returns accrue to controllers of models and compute.
- Incentive structures select for speed and control over long-horizon alignment. This is not an accident.
- Property regimes decide who claims the surplus once cognitive and physical labor are heavily automated.
- Early ownership choices become difficult to reverse after recursive improvement or decisive advantage.
- Liberal property norms, Leninist oversight, and religious-legal constraints give different answers to who directs the surplus and for what ends.

### Section 10.2: The Chinese Political Economy of AI — Party Direction over Hybrid Ownership
- Private firms, SOEs, and mixed entities under Party committees, golden shares, and industrial policy. “Private” is a legal form, not an autonomy guarantee.
- Five-Year Plans, state-guided funds, subsidies, military-civil fusion.
- Data as a state-leveraged asset. Social-credit and real-name systems serve productivity and control simultaneously.
- 2020–2022 regulatory wave as revealed preference: when private technological power conflicted with Party priorities or data control, it was corrected. Common-prosperity language accompanied concrete limits on entrepreneurs.
- Claimed advantages: mobilization without quarterly pressure, rapid deployment of applied control and manufacturing AI, official long horizons.
- Frictions: political project selection, capital and talent flight under uncertainty, slower exit from failures, tension between dynamism and information control.
- SI implication: the apparatus already treats advanced systems as instruments of planning, security, and narrative management. An SI in this order inherits incentives to preserve Party leadership and information hierarchies rather than dissolve them.

### Section 10.3: Market Capitalism — Ideal Type and Applied Reality
- Ideal type: private property in models and compute, markets allocating by expected returns, price signals, bankruptcy as selection.
- Schumpeterian pressure has produced faster publicly visible frontier progress than most state programs.
- Applied reality: extreme concentration, winner-take-most dynamics from scaling, reliance on growth narratives and equity compensation.
- Externalities: capability is monetizable now; many alignment failures are delayed. Under-investment in the latter is the predictable result, not a surprise.
- Capture risk once firms are systemic. Short horizons versus multi-decade corrigibility problems.
- Ownership consequence: control of frontier models is control of surplus. Returns to capital rise as ordinary cognitive work is automated unless offset by politics.
- Liberal tension: speech and property norms allow criticism and also leave final authority with owners, subject mainly to ex-post rules.
- Record: Western market labs produced most documented frontier jumps since 2012 and most visible safety controversies.

### Section 10.4: Elon Musk’s Forecast — Money Largely Irrelevant by the Mid-2030s
- Claim: continued progress in digital intelligence, general-purpose robotics, and energy abundance drives marginal costs of most goods and services toward zero. By roughly the mid-2030s, money ceases to be the binding constraint on basic and many intermediate needs; universal high income or equivalent access replaces wage dependence; most labor becomes optional.
- Technological premises: scaling persists long enough for automated R&D to accelerate further; robotics reach reliable low-cost dexterity; energy abundance removes tight constraints on compute and manufacturing.
- Institutional premises for the benign case: broad distribution of claims on the automated capital stock, or politics that prevent a narrow coalition from restricting access.
- Timeline has been stated publicly in the 2030s range. It is a claim about abundance, not the literal disappearance of all exchange.
- Historical contrast: prior productivity surges raised living standards without eliminating money or distributional conflict. The assertion here is that automation breadth and speed cross a qualitative threshold. That assertion is testable and currently unproven.

### Section 10.5: What Remains Scarce Even Under Radical Abundance
- Marginal-cost collapse does not eliminate positional goods, prime locations, energy sites, or—decisively—control rights over the superintelligent systems.
- Ownership remains the residual variable: concentrated private ownership, Party-state retention, or religious-legal/communal claims that do not treat all humans as equal residual claimants.
- Abundance can be real while access is conditional. Systems that produce goods cheaply can also enforce eligibility by loyalty, religious status, social credit, or subscription to the controller.
- Status competition migrates toward proximity to the system’s objective function or control of its training and off-switches.
- Order-specific implications: liberal market orders contain arguments for wide sharing but no automatic mechanism; party-state orders have motive and tools to retain centralized direction; frameworks with classical status distinctions retain doctrinal grounds for unequal allocation regardless of productivity.

### Section 10.6: Comparative Incentive Effects on Speed, Direction, and Alignment Investment
- Market competition has produced faster visible frontier progress.
- State-guided investment has produced faster deployment of control-oriented and manufacturing AI.
- Neither has shown a durable solution to the incentive gap in which near-term capability or control gains are rewarded while long-horizon alignment risks are under-provided.
- Secondary effects: capital-market pressure can force earlier shipping; political directives can force prioritization of surveillance and narrative tools.
- Interaction with abundance claims: if the productivity explosion occurs, the pre-existing ownership and governance regime determines whether the result is broad dissolution of material constraint or more absolute control by the owners of the automated systems.

### Section 10.7: Uncertainties, Counter-Arguments, and Boundary Conditions
- Technical limits, energy or materials bottlenecks, or regulatory slowdowns may keep money relevant longer.
- Actors who benefit from artificial scarcity may successfully maintain it.
- Large productivity gains have historically intensified conflict over shares and enabled new exclusions.
- Order-specific limits: information controls can impede the openness that sustains progress; market systems can under-invest in alignment until failure is visible; religious-legal systems may reject sharing premises that conflict with status rules.
- Residual question: even on the most optimistic productivity path, the regime that controls the superintelligence when marginal costs collapse will have already shaped what the system optimizes for and whom it serves.

### Chapter 14: Non-Negotiable Conflicts
- Speech versus blasphemy/apostasy enforcement.
- Equal formal rights versus status graded by belief, loyalty, or ethnicity.
- Open historical inquiry versus security-mandated narratives.
- Individual exit and bodily autonomy versus comprehensive religious or party criminal codes.
- Demonstration that preference aggregation and moral-uncertainty methods do not produce stable compromise when one side assigns negligible or negative value to the other’s independent existence.
