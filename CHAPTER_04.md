**Chapter 4: The Scaling Regime and Lab Divergence (2012–Present)**

The 2012 ImageNet result is the conventional marker for the current regime. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep convolutional network on two consumer NVIDIA GPUs and cut the top-5 error rate on a large visual recognition benchmark by a substantial margin. The technical ingredients were not individually new. Convolutional layers apply the same small set of learned filters across an image, detecting local patterns while keeping the number of parameters manageable. ReLU activations (rectified linear units) replace negative weighted sums with zero and leave positive sums unchanged; the simple nonlinearity trains more reliably in deep networks than the saturating sigmoids used earlier. Dropout randomly disables a fraction of units during each training step, forcing the network to learn redundant representations and reducing overfitting on the finite training set. GPU training exploits the parallel matrix arithmetic already present in graphics hardware to perform the millions of weight updates far faster than contemporary CPUs. Their combination at that scale demonstrated that further gains could be obtained by increasing model size, dataset size, and compute together. Subsequent years showed that the relationship was more regular than most observers had expected. Performance on a range of tasks continued to improve as those three factors grew, often in rough proportion to one another. The pattern came to be called scaling.

Scaling is an empirical regularity, not a law of nature. It has so far held across several orders of magnitude for language, vision, and multimodal models. It has not held uniformly for every task, and it has not eliminated the need for architectural choices, data quality, or post-training. It has, however, shifted the institutional requirements. Once returns to additional compute and data remain positive, the organizations that can assemble the largest clusters and the largest usable datasets gain a systematic advantage on the measured tasks. That advantage is not automatically an advantage in every downstream use, but it is large enough, and visible enough, that capital and talent have followed it.

Subsequent results supplied numerical illustrations of the same pattern. On language modeling, the original GPT-3 (2020) was trained with roughly 175 billion parameters and on the order of 300 billion tokens. A parameter is one of the adjustable numerical weights inside the network; a token is a unit of text (roughly a word or sub-word) used in training. Later models moved into the hundreds of billions and then the trillion-parameter range while training on trillions of tokens. Reported training compute for frontier runs rose from the low \(10^{23}\)–\(10^{24}\) FLOPs range around 2020 to the mid-to-high \(10^{25}\)–\(10^{26}\) FLOPs range by 2023–2024 for the largest publicly discussed systems. A FLOP is one floating-point operation; training compute is the total number of such operations performed during the entire training run. The relationship between compute and loss on held-out data has been approximately power-law over several orders of magnitude: each additional order of magnitude in training compute has produced a roughly constant fractional reduction in cross-entropy loss, holding architecture and data quality roughly fixed. Cross-entropy loss is the standard numerical measure of how far the model’s predicted probabilities diverge from the actual next token; lower is better. The exponent is less than 1, so returns diminish, yet they have remained positive and large enough to justify continued investment.

Vision and multimodal scaling followed parallel trajectories. ImageNet top-5 error (the rate at which the correct label is absent from the model’s five highest-probability guesses) fell from the mid-20 percent range before AlexNet to the low single digits for later convolutional and transformer models as both model size and pre-training data increased. Contrastive language-image models moved from hundreds of millions to billions of parameters and from tens of millions to billions of image-text pairs, with corresponding gains on zero-shot transfer (performance on tasks the model was not explicitly trained to perform). In each case the largest jumps occurred when compute, data, and parameter count were increased together rather than when any single factor was scaled in isolation.

The growth in required resources has been closer to exponential in calendar time than in any single technical variable. Cluster sizes moved from tens of GPUs in the early 2010s to thousands by the late 2010s and to tens of thousands of accelerators for the largest 2023–2025 training runs. Power draw for a single frontier training run rose from the low megawatts to the tens or low hundreds of megawatts. Capital expenditure on AI-related data-center infrastructure by the leading laboratories and their cloud partners moved from the low billions to the tens of billions of dollars per year. These are not pure technological exponentials; they are the joint product of continued positive returns to scale and of competitive and investor pressure to capture the next increment before rivals do. Where the measured returns have flattened on a given benchmark, laboratories have shifted to new benchmarks, longer-horizon tasks, or synthetic data rather than declaring the regime finished.

The numerical pattern is therefore double. On the tasks where scaling has been measured carefully, performance has improved as a power law in compute and data—predictable, diminishing, and still positive across the ranges so far tested. On the institutional side, the resources required to remain at the frontier have grown exponentially in calendar time. Public estimates place GPT-3 (2020) in the low \(10^{23}\) FLOPs range. By 2023–2024 the largest discussed training runs sat in the mid-to-high \(10^{25}\)–\(10^{26}\) FLOPs range—an increase of roughly two to three orders of magnitude in four years. That is consistent with a doubling time on the order of 6–10 months over the period, depending on which runs are counted. Early post-AlexNet experiments used one or two GPUs. By the late 2010s frontier runs used thousands of accelerators. By 2023–2025 the largest announced clusters contained tens of thousands of high-end GPUs or equivalent accelerators. Moving from ~2 GPUs (2012) to ~10,000+ GPUs (2024) is an increase of more than three orders of magnitude in a little over a decade. A single large training run that drew low-single-digit megawatts in the late 2010s now draws tens of megawatts, with announced multi-year campus plans in the 100 MW–1 GW range. Power demand for the largest runs has therefore risen by one to two orders of magnitude in roughly five years. Aggregate annual capital spending by the leading laboratories and their primary cloud partners on AI training infrastructure moved from the low single-digit billions of dollars around 2019–2020 to the tens of billions per year by 2024–2025. Order-of-magnitude growth in five years is exponential on any ordinary investment horizon. The first regularity is a property of current architectures and data. The second is a property of competition under that regularity. Neither regularity inserts liberal, Leninist, or religious-legal objectives into the trained weights. Both regularities amplify whatever objectives are present in the data and the post-training process.

The laboratories that captured most of the early gains operated under different formal structures and different actual constraints. Some began as academic projects or nonprofit research organizations and later raised large private capital. Others were founded inside or acquired by existing technology companies. Still others were created explicitly as commercial entities. In each case the public language emphasized scientific progress, safety, or broad benefit. The operational reality was competition for compute, talent, and data under investor or corporate timelines. Nonprofit status or founding charters did not remove the need to raise successive rounds or to demonstrate capability milestones that justified those rounds. Closed frontier models and selective open-weight releases both appeared. The former protected competitive position and allowed tighter control over deployment; the latter expanded the set of actors who could fine-tune or study systems close to the frontier. Export controls on advanced chips were introduced by the United States and allied governments. Enforcement has been partial. Chips, cloud access, and trained weights have continued to move across jurisdictions through legal purchases, intermediaries, and open releases.

Capital expenditure on data centers and specialized hardware rose sharply after 2018 and again after 2022. Training runs that once fit on a handful of GPUs moved to clusters measured in thousands and then tens of thousands of accelerators. Power and cooling became binding constraints alongside chip supply. Talent concentrated in a small number of laboratories and their immediate suppliers. The geographic distribution of the largest clusters remained heavily weighted toward the United States, with secondary concentrations in other liberal-market jurisdictions and a growing but still smaller set of state-directed facilities elsewhere. Open-weight models narrowed the gap on many benchmarks for organizations that could not train at the frontier, yet the largest closed systems continued to lead on the most demanding long-horizon and agentic evaluations. The gap has narrowed and widened in successive cycles; it has not disappeared.

The political question did not vanish under scaling. Larger models trained on broader data still acquire the regularities present in that data and the objectives reinforced during post-training. When laboratories apply human feedback, constitutional principles, or preference optimization, they shape outputs toward what their raters and rules reward. Those raters and rules reflect the employing institutions. A system trained and steered inside a market laboratory under U.S. or allied jurisdiction is not the same object as a system trained under mandatory party leadership, territorial narratives, or religious-legal constraints. Scaling amplifies whichever constraint set is applied. It does not generate a neutral intelligence that later receives values as an optional overlay.

Current leading indicators tracked by laboratories include performance on long-horizon agentic tasks, the rate at which systems complete research or engineering work previously reserved for specialists, and capability in cyber and scientific settings. These metrics are imperfect. High scores can coexist with brittleness off-distribution. Improvement remains fastest on whatever is reinforced. Organizations already treat certain capability levels as strategically relevant while describing the same systems publicly as narrow tools. Full recurrent self improvement without heavy human scaffolding has not been demonstrated. The interval between releases and measurable downstream gains has nonetheless shortened. Hardware and power plans now incorporate multi-year projections. This is not an intelligence explosion. It is evidence that increments continue to arrive under existing incentives, and that the institutions able to sustain the largest training runs are positioned to capture the next increments first.

The next chapter examines the alignment techniques that have been proposed and tested inside this regime. Scaling has made those techniques more urgent and, so far, has not made them sufficient. The systems are larger. The objectives they acquire remain those of the data, the loss, and the institutions that control both.
