**Chapter 3: Symbolic AI, Winters, and Parallel Programs (1956–2012)**

The 1956 Dartmouth workshop is conventionally treated as the founding event of artificial intelligence. John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon proposed a summer study on the conjecture that “every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.” The proposal was optimistic about both the technical problem and the institutional setting in which it would be solved. It assumed that intelligence could be formalized as rules and search, that the relevant knowledge could be captured by researchers, and that the resulting systems would be tools rather than autonomous agents with their own objectives. Those assumptions shaped the next two decades and then collided with results that the assumptions could not absorb.

Early symbolic systems produced impressive demonstrations on narrow problems. Logic Theorist, General Problem Solver, and later expert systems such as DENDRAL and MYCIN showed that carefully engineered rule bases could outperform non-specialists in restricted domains. Funding followed. The U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency and other military sponsors supported work that promised better logistics, intelligence analysis, and command systems. British and Japanese programs pursued similar goals under different national banners. The technical claim was that human expertise could be extracted, encoded, and then applied more consistently than the experts themselves. The political claim, rarely stated so baldly, was that the same extraction and encoding process could be directed by whichever institutions controlled the researchers and the computers.

The first AI winter arrived when the demonstrations failed to scale. Expert systems proved brittle outside their original domains, expensive to maintain, and dependent on continuous human knowledge engineering. The combinatorial explosion of search spaces and the difficulty of capturing tacit knowledge produced diminishing returns. Funding contracted in the United States and the United Kingdom in the mid-1970s and again in the late 1980s. The winter was real, but it was not a pure technical verdict. It was also a funding verdict. When near-term military or commercial returns failed to materialize, sponsors reduced support. The pattern would recur: capability claims that cannot be converted into operational advantage lose institutional priority.

Connectionism offered a different technical bet. Neural networks, largely sidelined after early critiques, revived in the 1980s with backpropagation and multilayer perceptrons. The approach treated learning as adjustment of weights rather than explicit rule writing. It required data and compute rather than hand-crafted knowledge bases. Early results were modest, but the method avoided some of the brittleness of pure symbolic systems. It also shifted the institutional requirements. Instead of expert knowledge engineers, the new approach needed large datasets and faster hardware. Those resources were more readily available in commercial and academic settings in the liberal West than in most state-directed programs of the period.

The mathematical core is linear algebra plus a nonlinearity at each layer. A multilayer perceptron consists of layers of units that compute weighted sums of their inputs and then pass the result through a nonlinear activation function. Training adjusts the weights to reduce the difference between the network’s output and a target value on labeled examples. Backpropagation, rediscovered and popularized in the mid-1980s, uses the chain rule to compute how each weight contributes to the final error and therefore how it should be changed. The update rule for any individual weight looks locally like a gradient step, which is why the procedure is sometimes blurred with ordinary regression. The overall mapping from inputs to outputs, however, is a composition of nonlinear functions, not a linear one. Without the nonlinearities, any number of stacked layers collapses to a single linear transformation no more powerful than logistic regression. With them, the network can form intermediate features that are nonlinear functions of the original inputs. A hidden unit can fire only when a particular combination of input features is present; later layers can then combine those intermediate detections. That extra capacity is the source of the additional representational power, and it is also why the learned weights become harder to interpret. The procedure is local and iterative. It does not require the designer to state symbolic rules; it requires a loss function, a set of examples, and enough computation to perform many small updates. The same machinery can be applied to vision, speech, or prediction tasks once the inputs and targets are represented as numbers. Because the model class is strictly larger than linear or logistic regression, the content of the training data and the loss function determine which regularities the network will capture. No separate ethical layer is inserted by the mathematics.

Early demonstrations were narrow but real. Networks learned to recognize handwritten digits, pronounce English text from orthography, and perform simple control tasks. They did not require an expert to enumerate every relevant feature in advance. Performance improved with more data and more hidden units, within the limits of the computers then available. The method also exposed new failure modes. Networks could memorize training examples without generalizing, especially when data were scarce. They could latch onto spurious correlations that happened to be present in the sample. And the learned weights were opaque: an engineer could inspect the numerical values but could not easily extract a compact verbal rule that explained why a particular input produced a particular output. The opacity was the price of the flexibility.

The same hardware trajectory that later powered large-scale training was already visible in the late 2000s, and the order of its uses is often inverted in popular accounts. NVIDIA GPUs were applied to neural-network training before they were applied to Bitcoin mining. The earliest documented experiments date to 2006, when Kumar Chellapilla implemented a convolutional network on a GPU. In 2009 Rajat Raina, Anand Madhavan, and Andrew Ng showed that contemporary graphics processors could train large-scale deep belief networks orders of magnitude faster than the CPUs then available; CUDA, released in 2006–2007, made the programming practical. The 2012 AlexNet result—Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton training a deep convolutional network on two GTX 580s—was the public demonstration that shifted the field, but it rested on prior GPU work rather than initiating it. GPU Bitcoin mining began later, in mid-2010, once rising network difficulty made CPU mining inefficient. Miners such as ArtForz and others moved to GPUs for hash-rate advantage. That transition occurred roughly four years after the first published GPU neural-network experiments and a year after the Raina–Madhavan–Ng results. The subsequent commercial mining boom increased demand for the same chips and raised their price, but it did not create the technical pathway. Academic groups had already established that the parallel matrix arithmetic required by multilayer networks mapped efficiently onto graphics hardware. The confusion that reverses the order is common because mining became economically conspicuous sooner, while the earlier research papers circulated mainly inside technical communities. The record is the opposite: the chips were first turned to weight updates on data, then to proof-of-work hashing.

These technical properties changed what institutions could build and maintain. Symbolic expert systems had needed continuous access to domain specialists who could articulate and revise rules. Neural systems needed collections of examples and machines that could process them. In the United States and Western Europe, commercial digitization, academic data-sharing practices, and the steady decline in the cost of computation supplied both. Defense and intelligence agencies also funded early neural work when it promised advantages in signal processing or pattern recognition, but the broader research community was not confined to classified programs. The principal academic work that revived multilayer networks and backpropagation was done in university groups. David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams published the key 1986 papers on backpropagation while based at UC San Diego and Carnegie Mellon. Terrence Sejnowski’s work on NETtalk (with Charles Rosenberg) was academic. James McClelland and the PDP research group operated in the same university setting. These efforts received support from standard federal sources of the period—primarily the Office of Naval Research, DARPA, and the National Science Foundation—along with some corporate funding. Defense agencies were interested in pattern recognition, signal processing, and adaptive systems, and they funded relevant work, but the published breakthroughs came from open academic laboratories. Intelligence-community interest existed, particularly in automated speech and image analysis, yet it did not produce the central algorithmic results or a publicly identified cohort of NSA researchers who drove the 1980s revival. Most of the classified or intelligence-directed work of that era remained in traditional signal-processing and expert-system approaches; neural methods were still experimental and data-hungry. In most centrally planned or tightly controlled environments of the same decades, large, diverse, and shareable datasets were harder to assemble, and the hardware required for repeated training runs was scarcer. The method therefore scaled first where data and compute could be accumulated without continuous political clearance for each new collection.

The revival did not eliminate the political question. A network trained on data selected and labeled by a given institution will reproduce the regularities present in that data, including any systematic omissions, biases, or prohibitions the institution has already imposed. If certain categories of examples are excluded, or if the loss function is shaped to penalize particular outputs, the resulting weights embed those choices. The fact that the rules are no longer written in explicit if-then form does not make them neutral. It merely makes them harder to audit. Early neural work already exhibited the pattern on a small scale: performance reflected the distribution of the training set more than any abstract notion of correctness. Later systems would exhibit it at far larger scale.

By the early 1990s the approach had produced usable systems in speech recognition, optical character recognition, and limited forecasting, yet it had not displaced symbolic methods across the board. Compute and data remained limiting. The theoretical guarantees were weak. And the institutional path that favored open data collection and iterative experimentation was still concentrated in the liberal West. The subsequent chapter shows how those limits shifted after 2012. The record through the 1980s and 1990s is already clear on one point: once learning is defined as weight adjustment on data, the objectives that shape the data and the loss become the objectives the system acquires. No separate ethical layer is inserted by the mathematics.

Parallel state programs revealed the same technical possibilities under different political constraints. Soviet research explored cybernetics and early machine translation as tools for economic planning and administrative coordination. Ideological suspicion delayed some lines of work, but the underlying goal remained consistent with party direction of information and resources. Chinese programs, smaller in scale, treated computation primarily as an instrument of state modernization and control rather than as an open scientific domain. Neither produced systems competitive with Western academic and commercial efforts in the period, yet both demonstrated that the same formal methods could be subordinated to planning and security objectives. The machines did not select their uses. The sponsoring institutions did.

By the early 2010s the technical pieces for a new regime were accumulating. Larger datasets, faster processors, and incremental improvements in neural architectures had not yet produced the dramatic capability jumps that would arrive after 2012, but they had already shown that statistical learning on data could outperform hand-crafted rules on many tasks. The institutional pieces were also in place. Western laboratories operated under mixed academic, commercial, and military funding with residual space for publication and criticism. State programs elsewhere continued to treat advanced computing as an extension of national planning and security. Nothing in the record of the symbolic era or the first neural revival suggested that greater capability would erase these differences. Greater capability would amplify whichever objectives the controlling institutions chose to reward.

The next chapter examines the scaling regime that followed. The decades from 1956 to 2012 established two durable facts. First, formal systems can be made to perform narrow intellectual tasks at or above human specialist level when the problem is sufficiently constrained. Second, the objectives those systems serve are those of their sponsors. Winters arrived when the sponsors judged the returns insufficient. They did not arrive because the underlying political question—who directs the machines—had been resolved.
