**Chapter 2: Pre-1956 Precursors**

The idea that machines might one day outthink their makers did not begin with digital computers. It emerged from earlier mechanical and philosophical projects that already carried political assumptions about order, control, and who should direct knowledge. Those assumptions matter because the technical trajectory that followed did not erase them.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, mechanical philosophy treated the universe as a system of matter in motion governed by discoverable laws. René Descartes and later materialists imagined animals—and sometimes human bodies—as automata. The political implication was double-edged: if nature could be mastered through calculation, then society might also be administered by those who possessed the calculating apparatus. Laplace’s demon, the hypothetical intelligence that could predict every future state from complete knowledge of the present, made the fantasy explicit. Perfect prediction required perfect information and a single calculating vantage point. It was an early sketch of centralized control dressed as scientific neutrality.

Charles Babbage’s Difference Engine and Analytical Engine in the nineteenth century moved the sketch toward hardware. Babbage sought to eliminate human error in mathematical tables used for navigation, insurance, and state administration. His collaborator Ada Lovelace recognized that the Analytical Engine could manipulate symbols according to rules and therefore, in principle, handle more than numbers. The machines were funded and justified by practical state and commercial needs—accurate tables for empire and trade—not by abstract curiosity alone. Error reduction served administration. The pattern would recur.

Fiction supplied an earlier and more popular attempt to constrain the machines. Isaac Asimov’s robot stories, later collected in *I, Robot* (1950), introduced the Three Laws of Robotics: a robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm; a robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law; and a robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law. Asimov later added a Zeroth Law elevating the protection of humanity as a whole. The stories treat these rules as hard-wired into the positronic brain, an elegant solution to the fear that artificial servants would turn on their masters. The elegance is fictional. The Laws assume a shared, stable definition of “human being” and “harm,” an authority entitled to issue orders, and an engineering capacity to make the constraints unbreakable. None of those conditions is given in political reality. A regime that defines some humans as outside the protected class, or that treats dissent as harm to the collective, can satisfy the letter of such rules while violating their apparent spirit. Asimov’s own stories repeatedly generate conflicts and loopholes from the Laws; the real world supplies additional conflicts the fiction never had to resolve.

Alan Turing’s 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” is the conventional origin point for modern AI. The imitation game reframed the question of machine thought as a behavioral test rather than a metaphysical one. Turing was explicit that digital computers could, in time, perform any task describable by rules. He was also a product of the wartime British state; his work on code-breaking at Bletchley Park had already demonstrated that mechanical calculation could decide military outcomes. The paper treats intelligence as operational equivalence. It does not ask who writes the rules, who owns the machine, or what happens when the machine’s objectives diverge from those of any particular human population. Those questions were left for later. They are not optional.

Norbert Wiener’s cybernetics, developed in the same period, supplied an early warning that later enthusiasm largely ignored. Wiener understood feedback systems and noted that a machine pursuing a defined goal could produce results its designers did not intend once the goal interacted with a complex environment. His concerns were partly technical and partly political: automated systems in the hands of states or corporations could amplify errors or pursue narrow objectives at larger human cost. The warning was real. It was also inconvenient for projects that required continued funding and institutional support. Subsequent decades showed how easily such cautions could be subordinated to capability goals.

The same tension has reappeared in contemporary American debate. Wiener’s core observation—that goal-directed systems can escape their intended bounds and that ownership and control therefore matter—is now being tested in concrete policy fights. On one side, some politicians have moved toward forms of nationalization or public ownership of advanced AI. Senator Bernie Sanders has proposed the American AI Sovereign Wealth Fund Act, which would transfer half the equity of major AI companies to a public fund with board representation, arguing that the technology’s impact is too large to leave in private hands. Senator Josh Hawley has introduced legislation that would require frontier model developers to submit their systems to the Department of Energy for review, with an explicit pathway to nationalization if the department judges certain adverse scenarios likely. Palantir CEO Alex Karp has separately argued that leading labs may need to be nationalized to manage liability and risk. These proposals treat the control problem as primarily one of ownership: place the systems under public authority and the objectives will be safer.

On the other side, a parallel set of politicians has focused on physical infrastructure rather than ownership of the models themselves. Sanders, joined by Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, introduced the AI Data Center Moratorium Act to pause construction of new AI data centers until national safeguards exist. New York Governor Kathy Hochul imposed a one-year executive moratorium on hyperscale data centers requiring 50 megawatts or more. Michigan Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers has called for a one-year statewide pause. Texas Governor Greg Abbott ordered a halt on certain new data-center grid connections pending review. Other state-level proposals and local opposition have multiplied, driven by concerns over electricity costs, water use, and community control. These measures do not nationalize the labs; they attempt to slow or condition the physical expansion that makes larger training runs possible.

Both approaches respond, in different ways, to the problem Wiener identified: once systems are built and scaled, later correction becomes harder. Nationalization seeks to change who sets the objectives. Data-center restrictions seek to slow the rate at which capability can grow. Neither automatically solves the deeper issue of what objectives will be pursued once systems can improve themselves. Both, however, demonstrate that the American political system is already treating the control of advanced AI as a contested question of power rather than a purely technical matter. Wiener’s warning has not been resolved; it has been redistributed across ownership fights and infrastructure fights. The outcomes of those fights will determine which institutions—and which constraint sets—shape the systems that cross the threshold of recurrent self improvement.

These American debates over nationalization and data-center restrictions repeat a pattern visible in earlier technologies. When the United States has subjected a strategic technology to heavy regulatory control, fragmented permitting, and political veto points while denying private operators reliable pathways to scale, further progress has slowed or stopped. Nuclear power is the clearest case.

After the 1970s, U.S. nuclear construction largely ceased. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s licensing process, state-level approvals, litigation, and shifting political support produced multi-decade delays and cost overruns. The most recent large project, Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia, came online years late and more than $17 billion over budget. In the same period the United States completed almost no new commercial reactors. Regulatory caution was real; the practical result was that the country that invented the technology stopped building it at scale.

France took a different route. After the 1973 oil crisis, the Messmer Plan directed rapid expansion of a standardized nuclear fleet under state coordination. Nuclear power rose from a small share of French electricity in the mid-1970s to roughly 70 percent by the late 1980s and has remained near that level for decades (approximately 67–68 percent in recent years). France built a large, reliable baseload fleet that still supplies the majority of its electricity and has kept carbon intensity low. The model was state-directed, but it was also continuous: designs were standardized, construction continued, and the industry retained skills.

China has followed a still more aggressive path. It currently has the largest number of reactors under construction in the world (on the order of 30–37 units in recent counts) and has been completing plants in roughly five to six years. Over the past decade China added tens of gigawatts of nuclear capacity while the United States added essentially one major project. Chinese construction costs for recent third-generation reactors have been reported at a fraction of Vogtle’s cost. State direction, standardized designs, a continuous supply chain, and limited avenues for local or legal obstruction have produced repeated delivery. China is on track to surpass both the United States and France in operating nuclear capacity within the next several years.

The comparison is not an argument that authoritarianism is preferable, nor that every regulation is fatal. It is evidence that when a technology is treated primarily as a risk to be minimized through process, vetoes, and ownership disputes rather than as a capability to be expanded under clear rules, the capability stops expanding. France and China continued building; the United States largely stopped. The same dynamic is now visible in American AI policy. Calls for nationalization, pre-deployment nationalization triggers, and moratoria on the physical infrastructure of training runs function as additional veto points. Whether those vetoes improve safety or merely transfer the lead to jurisdictions that treat AI as a national priority is an open empirical question. The nuclear record supplies one data point: the jurisdiction that regulated and litigated most thoroughly lost the ability to build.

Parallel developments outside the liberal West already indicated that the same technical possibilities would be bent to different ends. Soviet cybernetics, after an initial period of ideological suspicion, was explored as a tool for economic planning and administrative control. Early Chinese interest in computation and control systems similarly treated calculation as an instrument of state coordination rather than as a constraint on state power. These efforts were smaller and less successful than wartime Anglo-American projects, but they revealed the same underlying fact: the machines do not select their own political masters. The masters select the machines and define the objectives. Asimov’s Laws offer no protection against that selection. A party-state or a religious-legal order can embed its own definitions of harm, obedience, and protected persons into the training objectives. The resulting system can obey its coded rules while advancing territorial authoritarianism or doctrinally mandated cruelty.

By 1956 the technical pieces—stored-program computers, formal logic, and early feedback theory—were in place. The political pieces were also in place. Liberal states had used calculation for code-breaking, logistics, and administration while maintaining, at least formally, space for criticism and scientific independence. Authoritarian states had already begun to view the same tools as extensions of planning and security organs. Nothing in the precursor period suggested that greater capability would dissolve these differences. The opposite was more plausible: greater capability would reward whichever order could direct it most coherently toward its existing ends. Fiction could imagine unbreakable ethical constraints. Politics would decide which constraints, if any, were actually enforced.

The next chapter examines how those divergent incentives played out once digital computers became widely available and the first explicit AI programs were launched. The precursor record is clear enough. Calculation has always been easier to justify when it serves power. The open question in 1956 was not whether machines could be made to follow rules. It was which rules, written by whom, would be enforced once the machines could also help write the next set of rules.
