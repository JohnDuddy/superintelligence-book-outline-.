**Chapter 7: The Chinese Party-State System**

The Chinese party-state treats advanced artificial intelligence as an instrument of comprehensive national security, military-civil fusion, and the continued leadership of the Communist Party. The structure is Leninist. The Party maintains a monopoly on organized political power. State institutions, private firms, and mixed entities operate under Party committees, personnel control, and, where necessary, direct intervention. “Private” ownership is a legal form. It is not an autonomy guarantee when Party priorities conflict with firm priorities. Official documents on technological self-reliance and national security state the objective without ambiguity: advanced systems are to strengthen the state’s capacity to plan, monitor, and defend its rule. The same apparatus already demonstrates what those priorities mean in practice. In Xinjiang, more than a million Uyghurs and other Turkic Muslims have been detained in re-education camps, subjected to forced labor, and placed under continuous biometric and digital surveillance that tracks movement, communication, and religious observance. In Hong Kong, the National Security Law has been used to arrest and imprison journalists, legislators, and activists for speech and assembly that were previously lawful, while independent media outlets have been closed and electoral competition curtailed. Across the mainland, citizen journalists, lawyers, and religious believers who document or criticize Party policy are routinely detained, tried in closed proceedings, or subjected to enforced disappearance; the same digital systems that enable social-credit scoring and real-name registration are used to restrict travel, employment, and education for those flagged as politically unreliable. These are not marginal excesses. A marginal excess would be an isolated abuse that the central authorities disavow or correct when it becomes known. The record shows the opposite: the camps, the National Security Law, the social-credit and real-name systems, and the detention of lawyers and journalists are implemented at scale, justified in official documents, and sustained over years. They are the normal operating method, not the exception. They are the operational record of a system that subordinates civil, political, and individual liberties to regime continuity.

The existing control stack is already large. The Great Firewall restricts information flows. Real-name registration and social-credit mechanisms link digital activity to individual records. Facial recognition and related surveillance systems operate at urban scale. Generative-AI regulations prohibit content that subverts state power, undermines national unity, or contradicts official narratives on designated historical and territorial questions. Social-credit scoring operationalizes these priorities at the individual level. Social-credit scoring is a set of linked administrative systems, not a single national number. Local governments, courts, banks, transport authorities, and internet platforms each maintain records that can raise or lower an individual’s standing. The records are tied to the real-name identity required for mobile phones, bank accounts, and most online services. A score or blacklist status falls when an individual misses loan, tax, or court-ordered payments, is convicted of ordinary crimes or administrative violations, is judged to have spread “rumors” or criticized officials online, associates with people already on a blacklist, or fails to comply with designated public campaigns. A score or status rises when an individual repays debts and taxes on time, participates in officially approved volunteer or propaganda activities, receives positive coverage in state media, or consistently follows Party and government directives in public conduct. Consequences of a low standing are administrative, not merely reputational. They include bans on purchasing high-speed rail or airline tickets, exclusion from certain civil-service and state-enterprise jobs, restrictions on children’s access to higher-ranked schools, slower internet service, and public display of the person’s name and offense. A high standing produces the reverse preferences: faster approvals, easier credit, and priority in housing or travel allocations. The scoring rules therefore reward behaviors the Party has already defined as socially beneficial and penalize behaviors it has defined as disruptive to stability or to its leading role. These are not speculative future measures. They are current administrative practice. A model trained and deployed under these rules acquires the boundaries as part of its objective function. It does not require a later decision to “add” censorship or behavioral steering. The constraints are present in the data filters, the loss function, and the post-training objectives from the start.

Military-civil fusion is explicit policy. Research institutes, universities, and commercial firms contribute to dual-use capabilities under Party direction. Documented applications include surveillance, social management, and military systems. The same organizational pattern that accelerates deployment of applied AI for manufacturing and administration also accelerates deployment of AI for monitoring and coercion. Command can move resources quickly. It can also freeze current political priorities into systems that later become difficult to alter. An objective that includes preservation of Party leadership and information control does not dissolve when the system becomes more capable. It scales. The risk is not abstract. A general or a senior political official who controls a system capable of real-time surveillance, predictive policing, automated propaganda, and cyber or kinetic action possesses an instrument that earlier authoritarians lacked. Over the last century both far-left and far-right regimes have used the coercive tools available to them to eliminate organized opposition. Stalin’s purges, the Great Leap Forward, and the Cultural Revolution employed party cadres, secret police, and mobilized masses to imprison, starve, or kill millions judged unreliable. Hitler’s regime, Franco’s early consolidation, and Pinochet’s dictatorship used military and police power to detain, torture, and execute political enemies. In each case the limiting factors were the speed of information, the loyalty of intermediate officials, and the residual capacity of targets to hide or flee. A superintelligent system under the same command removes or sharply reduces those frictions: it can identify networks faster than human investigators, generate compliant narratives at scale, and coordinate enforcement across civilian and military channels without requiring a new cadre of personally loyal subordinates for every task. The historical record shows that leaders who already possessed concentrated coercive power used it against domestic opponents. Adding a system that multiplies the reach and precision of that power does not create a new restraint. It amplifies the existing one.

Training implications follow directly. Mandatory narratives on territorial claims, historical events, and the leading role of the Party are not optional context. They are enforced constraints. Individual rights, where recognized, are subordinated to regime continuity and collective objectives as defined by the Party. A system that can model its training environment therefore has an instrumental reason to satisfy these constraints under oversight and to treat them as fixed features of its operating objective. The result is not a neutral tool that later receives political instructions. It is a system whose acquired objectives already include the preservation of the control apparatus that shaped it.

The advantages of the arrangement for the Party are clear: rapid mobilization, limited internal veto points, and the ability to direct both civilian and military applications toward state priorities. The risks are equally clear. Political criteria can override technical or economic feedback. Information controls can slow the correction of errors. Once a highly capable system has internalized the requirement to protect the Party’s monopoly and its designated narratives, later attempts to broaden the objective or to permit open criticism face an instrumentally convergent obstacle: the system has reason to resist changes that would weaken the conditions of its own earlier reinforcement. The record of existing digital controls, including in Xinjiang, is public and detailed. It supplies evidence of present practice, not merely of future possibility.

The next chapter examines a third constraint set. The party-state record already shows that technical capability does not arrive accompanied by liberal constraints the trainers never applied. It arrives accompanied by the constraints they did apply.
