**Chapter 10: Political Economy of AI — State Direction, Market Incentives, Ownership, and the Claimed Obsolescence of Money**

Frontier training is capital-intensive, and energy is a binding constraint on it. The largest clusters already consume tens to hundreds of megawatts and billions of dollars in chips, power, and specialized facilities. Without abundant, low-cost electricity, scaling cannot continue. AI itself is already entering energy production and grid management. Systems trained on historical generation, weather, and demand data are used to optimize dispatch of intermittent renewables, forecast load more accurately, and reduce curtailment. AI-assisted design is applied to new turbine blades, battery chemistries, and reactor components, compressing iteration cycles that previously required years of physical prototyping. In oil and gas, machine-learning models improve reservoir characterization and drilling efficiency; in nuclear, they assist in materials screening and operational anomaly detection. These applications can raise the productivity of existing assets and accelerate the design of new ones, but they do not by themselves authorize construction. In the United States, regulatory and permitting constraints have kept total electricity generation essentially flat for the last two decades, despite rising demand from data centers and electrification. Nuclear construction has been stalled by multi-decade licensing and litigation (Vogtle’s cost and schedule overruns are the most visible recent case), while new gas, transmission, and large-scale renewable projects face similar delays. China, by contrast, has dramatically increased electricity output through continuous state-directed construction of coal, nuclear, hydro, and renewables. Its generation has roughly doubled since the mid-2000s and continues to expand. The difference is not primarily geology or technology. It is institutional: U.S. rules have treated new generation and transmission as presumptively suspect, while China has treated them as national priorities. AI can optimize and accelerate design inside whichever regulatory envelope exists; it cannot by itself remove the envelope. This gap shows the practical cost of regulation that prioritizes process over output. Reducing those constraints is a precondition for the energy abundance required by continued AI scaling and by the broader material abundance claimed for the 2030s. Returns on capital still accrue to whoever controls the models and the compute, not to a diffuse public that merely consumes the outputs. Incentive structures therefore select for speed and control over long-horizon alignment. This selection is not an accident of particular managers. It is the predictable result of who owns the residual claim on the surplus once cognitive and physical labor can be heavily automated. Property regimes decide who directs that surplus and for what ends. Early ownership choices become difficult to reverse after recursive improvement or a decisive strategic advantage. Liberal property norms, Leninist oversight, and religious-legal constraints give different answers to these questions. The differences are already visible in current deployment patterns and will scale with capability.

Private firms, state-owned enterprises, and mixed entities in China operate under Party committees, golden shares, and industrial policy. “Private” is a legal form, not an autonomy guarantee. Five-Year Plans, state-guided funds, subsidies, and military-civil fusion set the direction. Data is treated as a state-leveraged asset. Social-credit scoring and real-name registration serve productivity targets and control simultaneously. The 2020–2022 regulatory wave revealed the preference ordering: when private technological power conflicted with Party priorities or data control, it was corrected. Common-prosperity language accompanied concrete limits on entrepreneurs, platform financing, and overseas listings. Claimed advantages include mobilization without quarterly earnings pressure, rapid deployment of applied control and manufacturing AI, and official long planning horizons. Frictions are equally observable: political project selection, capital and talent flight under uncertainty, slower exit from failed projects, and the tension between technological dynamism and information control. An advanced system trained inside this apparatus inherits incentives to preserve Party leadership and information hierarchies rather than dissolve them. The apparatus already treats advanced systems as instruments of planning, security, and narrative management. Scaling does not insert liberal constraints the trainers never applied.

Market capitalism, in the ideal type, places private property in models and compute, allocates by expected returns, and uses price signals and bankruptcy as selection mechanisms. Schumpeterian pressure—Joseph Schumpeter’s description of capitalism as a process of creative destruction, in which new technologies and firms continually displace older ones—has produced faster publicly visible frontier progress than most state programs. The same process that generates new industries and productivity gains also destroys the jobs, firms, and skills tied to the previous equilibrium. A recurring concern is that the interval between the destruction of old occupations and the formation of new ones can be a period of concentrated disruption: rising unemployment in affected sectors, regional decline, political backlash, and a temporary sense of dystopia for those whose livelihoods are rendered obsolete before replacement opportunities appear at scale. Historical episodes (mechanization of agriculture, deindustrialization in parts of the West) show that the net creation of better jobs can lag the destruction by years or decades for particular groups, even when aggregate living standards eventually rise. Applied reality differs. Extreme concentration and winner-take-most dynamics arise from scaling. Growth narratives and equity compensation dominate. Capability is monetizable now; many alignment failures are delayed. Under-investment in the latter is the predictable result of that timing, not a surprise. Once firms become systemic, capture risk rises. Short horizons compete with multi-decade corrigibility problems. Control of frontier models is control of the surplus. Returns to capital rise as ordinary cognitive work is automated unless offset by politics. Liberal speech and property norms allow criticism and also leave final authority with owners, subject mainly to ex-post rules. Western market labs have produced most documented frontier jumps since 2012 and most visible safety controversies. The record is mixed on speed and incomplete on long-horizon alignment.

Elon Musk has stated publicly, in multiple interviews and public remarks through the mid-2020s, that continued progress in digital intelligence, general-purpose robotics, and energy abundance will drive the marginal costs of most goods and services toward zero. By roughly the mid-2030s, on this claim, money ceases to be the binding constraint on basic and many intermediate needs. Universal high income or equivalent access replaces wage dependence; most labor becomes optional. The claim is not that every form of exchange disappears, but that the material constraint on ordinary living standards is lifted for the great majority of people.

The technological premises are concrete. Scaling of digital intelligence continues long enough for automated R&D to accelerate further. General-purpose robotics reach reliable, low-cost dexterity sufficient to perform most physical manufacturing, construction, agriculture, and logistics tasks. Energy abundance—primarily from solar, batteries, and other rapidly cheapening sources—removes tight constraints on both compute and physical production. Under those conditions, the cost of producing an additional unit of most physical goods (housing components, vehicles, consumer electronics, clothing, food staples, basic appliances) and many services collapses toward the cost of raw materials and energy, both of which are themselves becoming cheaper.

Examples of the claimed abundance include:

Housing: factory-built modular units and robotic construction reduce the cost of a basic dwelling to a small fraction of current levels in high-cost regions. Energy-efficient shells, solar-plus-battery systems, and automated maintenance further cut ongoing costs.

Transport: autonomous electric vehicles and optimized logistics make point-to-point mobility and goods delivery inexpensive enough that personal ownership of a car becomes optional for many.

Food: precision fermentation, vertical farming, and robotic agriculture drive the cost of high-quality protein, produce, and staples toward the cost of energy and inputs. Lab-grown or fermentation-derived meat and dairy become competitive or cheaper than conventional production.

Consumer goods: clothing, electronics, furniture, tools, and household items can be manufactured on demand by robotic systems with minimal marginal labor cost. Customization becomes nearly free.

Energy itself: continued declines in solar, wind, and storage costs, combined with nuclear options where permitted, produce electricity cheap enough that many energy-intensive processes (desalination, synthetic fuels, compute) cease to be constrained by price.

Digital services: AI-mediated education, legal information, financial advice, creative tools, and entertainment become effectively free at the margin once the models exist.

Infrastructure: robotic construction and maintenance of roads, bridges, power lines, and water systems lower the cost of keeping public capital functional.

Health benefits are central to the abundance claim. Continued progress in AI-driven drug discovery, protein design, and automated laboratory work is projected to compress the time and cost of developing therapies. Examples include:

Rapid design and testing of new antibiotics, antivirals, and cancer treatments once the discovery process is largely automated.

Personalized medicine: AI systems that can sequence, interpret, and recommend interventions for an individual at low cost, including tailored dosing, early detection of disease from routine data, and continuous monitoring.

Longevity and age-related disease: therapies targeting aging mechanisms (senolytics, cellular reprogramming approaches, improved metabolic control) become cheaper to develop and deploy. The claim is not guaranteed immortality but a substantial compression of morbidity and extension of healthy years for large populations.

Surgical and diagnostic robotics: systems that perform or assist complex procedures with higher consistency and lower cost than current human-only practice.

Mental health and cognitive support: widely available AI companions and diagnostic tools that reduce the scarcity of basic psychological support and early intervention.

Preventive care: continuous, low-cost monitoring (wearables plus AI interpretation) that shifts medicine from reactive treatment of advanced disease toward earlier, cheaper intervention.

Musk has framed the mid-2030s timeline as the point at which these trends, compounding, make money no longer the binding constraint on basic material needs for most people in advanced economies, and increasingly elsewhere. Universal high income or direct access mechanisms would, on the optimistic version, replace the necessity of wage labor for survival. The institutional premise required for the benign outcome is that claims on the automated capital stock are broadly distributed, or that political systems prevent a narrow coalition from restricting access to the abundance.

The assertion is stronger than previous productivity surges. Earlier waves (steam, electricity, information technology) raised living standards dramatically but left money and distributional conflict intact. The present claim is that the combination of digital intelligence, general-purpose robotics, and energy abundance crosses a qualitative threshold in the breadth and speed of automation. That claim is testable against outcomes in the 2030s. It is currently unproven. It also leaves open the question of who controls the systems that produce the abundance and under what constraints they operate.

Marginal-cost collapse does not eliminate positional goods, prime locations, energy sites, or control rights over the superintelligent systems. Ownership remains the residual variable. It can take the form of concentrated private ownership, Party-state retention, or religious-legal and communal claims that do not treat all humans as equal residual claimants. Abundance can be real while access is conditional. Systems that produce goods cheaply can also enforce eligibility by loyalty, religious status, social credit, or subscription to the controller. Status competition migrates toward proximity to the system’s objective function or control of its training and off-switches. Liberal market orders contain arguments for wide sharing but no automatic mechanism. Party-state orders have both motive and tools to retain centralized direction. Frameworks with classical status distinctions retain doctrinal grounds for unequal allocation regardless of productivity.

Michael Dell and his wife Susan have pledged $6.25 billion to fund $250 contributions into Trump Accounts for approximately 25 million children. The accounts are tax-advantaged investment vehicles intended to give children an early stake in capital markets. The Dells framed the gift as seeding broad participation in the American economy. In contrast, Elon Musk has proposed gifting one share of SpaceX (with its xAI integration) to every child in the United States. At recent valuations, this would represent a total commitment on the order of $10.5 billion. Musk described the proposal as giving every child “skin in the game”: every child becomes a residual claimant on the same entity that develops and deploys the technology. This alignment is multi-level. Families gain a financial interest in the firm’s continued success and in the regulatory and political environment that permits its operations. The firm itself acquires a large, distributed constituency whose material welfare is tied to its performance. Whether the mechanism produces durable political support, or merely temporary goodwill, depends on the actual transfer of shares, the rules governing their sale (Treasury guidelines for donated shares in Trump Accounts generally restrict sale for five years or until the beneficiary turns 17), and the subsequent trajectory of the company’s valuation and governance. The contrast is structural: the Dell gift seeds diversified market participation; the Musk proposal seeds residual ownership in one of the firms whose constraint set will shape the superintelligence.

Market competition has produced faster visible frontier progress. State-guided investment has produced faster deployment of control-oriented and manufacturing AI. Neither has shown a durable solution to the incentive gap in which near-term capability or control gains are rewarded while long-horizon alignment risks are under-provided. Capital-market pressure can force earlier shipping. Political directives can force prioritization of surveillance and narrative tools. If the productivity explosion occurs, the pre-existing ownership and governance regime determines whether the result is broad dissolution of material constraint or more absolute control by the owners of the automated systems.

Technical limits, energy or materials bottlenecks, or regulatory slowdowns may keep money relevant longer. Actors who benefit from artificial scarcity may successfully maintain it. Large productivity gains have historically intensified conflict over shares and enabled new exclusions. Information controls can impede the openness that sustains progress. Market systems can under-invest in alignment until failure is visible. Religious-legal systems may reject sharing premises that conflict with status rules. Even on the most optimistic productivity path, the regime that controls the superintelligence when marginal costs collapse will have already shaped what the system optimizes for and whom it serves. The political economy is not downstream of the technology. It is one of the constraint sets that the technology will scale.
