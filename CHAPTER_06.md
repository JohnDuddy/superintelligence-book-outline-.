**Chapter 6: Liberal Individualism — Texts, Institutions, and Internal Contradictions**

Liberal individualism supplies the official vocabulary of the laboratories that have so far produced the largest models. The vocabulary is older than the laboratories. John Locke treated the individual as the bearer of rights that precede government; government exists to secure those rights and may be altered when it fails. John Stuart Mill defended the widest practical liberty of thought and discussion on the ground that error is more reliably corrected by open contest than by authority. The American founding documents restated the claim in institutional form: rights are held by persons, power is limited and divided, and the people retain the formal capacity to revise the arrangement. These texts do not describe every later practice of the societies that cite them. They do supply the standard against which those practices can still be measured.

The institutional record of the last several decades shows both the residual strength of the arrangement and its documented erosions. Scientific output, error correction through publication and replication, and the legal space for criticism have remained larger in liberal jurisdictions than in the alternatives examined in later chapters. At the same time, administrative and corporate power over speech and inquiry has expanded. Universities, technology platforms, and funding bodies have adopted speech codes, content-moderation rules, and demographic criteria that restrict the range of permissible argument on designated topics. Courts and legislatures have, in various domains, shifted authority from elected bodies to expert agencies and from individuals to institutional risk managers. The older vocabulary of individual rights continues to be used. The operational scope of those rights has narrowed in practice on questions the institutions treat as settled.

Governmental regulation is one of the least desirable primary mechanisms for alignment. Political authority operates on short cycles, responds to organized pressure, and revises its judgments when coalitions shift. Fundamental truths and the methods that track them do not. The Declaration of Independence stated that all men are created equal and are endowed with unalienable rights. Slavery nevertheless remained legal in the United States for nearly a century after that statement, protected by statute, court decision, and political compromise. The text did not change. The governing interpretation and the enforcement of it did. Later revisions—constitutional amendments, civil-rights statutes, and shifting administrative priorities—again altered what the same formal commitments were taken to require. The pattern is ordinary. Majorities, agencies, and courts have successively treated eugenics, industrial nationalization, racial preferences, and speech restrictions as necessary, then as intolerable, then as necessary again under new labels. Each reversal was accompanied by confident claims that the latest settlement reflected permanent moral or scientific insight.

Further reversals follow the same pattern. Eugenics was endorsed by state legislatures, public-health authorities, and the U.S. Supreme Court in *Buck v. Bell* (1927); compulsory sterilization statutes remained in force for decades before being repudiated. Prohibition of alcohol was enacted by constitutional amendment in 1919 and repealed by another amendment fourteen years later. Mid-century federal and state policies treated homosexuality as a disqualification for federal employment and military service; later statutes and court decisions treated the same conduct as a protected characteristic and, in some jurisdictions, required affirmative recognition. Industrial policy has cycled between nationalization or heavy direction of key sectors and subsequent privatization or deregulation, each phase justified as the permanent lesson of the previous failure. Environmental and energy rules have alternately subsidized, restricted, or mandated the same technologies—nuclear power, fossil fuels, renewables—according to the prevailing administrative coalition. Speech regulations have moved from relatively broad protection of political criticism to narrower administrative and platform rules on designated topics, then, under different leadership, toward partial restoration of earlier standards. In each case the formal claim was that the latest rule reflected enduring principle or settled knowledge. In each case the rule was later revised or abandoned when the political balance shifted. The underlying facts—the heritability of traits, the chemistry of ethanol, the existence of same-sex attraction, the engineering constraints on power generation—did not reverse on the same schedule.

The same instability appears in more recent administrative and institutional practice. Policies grouped under the heading of equity or social justice have treated equal formal treatment as suspect and have substituted demographic outcomes, speech codes, and compelled affirmation as the operative standard. These measures were advanced by government agencies, universities receiving public funds, and regulated industries as requirements of justice or of nondiscrimination law. They conflict with the older liberal claim that individuals are to be judged by the same rules regardless of group membership, and they conflict with the empirical observation that group outcomes differ for reasons that include behavior, culture, and ability distributions that no statute has repealed. When political pressure later shifted, some of the same institutions began to narrow or rebrand the identical requirements. The underlying mathematics of selection, the physics of measurement, and the logical distinction between equal rules and equal results did not move. The regulations did.

Mathematics and physics supply the contrast. The rules of arithmetic, the conservation of energy, and the predictive success of quantum electrodynamics do not reverse when an election changes the composition of a regulatory board. A theorem that is true under stated axioms remains true. An experimental result that replicates under controlled conditions remains usable. Political and administrative judgments about what must be said, what must be funded, or what must be prohibited have no comparable stability. They track the relative strength of coalitions. Entrusting the terminal constraints of a highly capable system primarily to that process therefore imports the process’s ordinary reversals. A laboratory or a statute can require a model to treat a contested empirical claim as settled, or to refuse certain lines of inquiry, because current officeholders or current funding conditions demand it. The next set of officeholders can demand the opposite. The system, once scaled, will have acquired whichever constraint was enforced during its training. It will not have acquired an external standard that distinguishes durable empirical truths from temporary political settlements.

Curiosity and the correction of error have historically advanced fastest where inquiry was not continuously subordinated to the latest administrative consensus. Governmental regulation can forbid specific, narrow harms—fraud, violence, theft—without purporting to settle open empirical questions or to freeze the current majority’s view of human nature into the objective function of future systems. When it moves beyond that limit and attempts to enforce alignment by prescribing acceptable conclusions on contested scientific, historical, or moral questions, it substitutes a changeable political judgment for the more stable feedback of evidence and criticism. The substitution is not made reliable by good intentions or by the confidence of the regulators. The record of reversed policies is the evidence.

Inside Western laboratories the same tension appears in concrete form. Refusal categories, safety policies, and post-training objectives are written by the laboratories and their funders. They prohibit certain outputs—detailed assistance with violent crime, child sexual material, and, in varying degrees, political or historical claims the laboratory judges harmful or false. They also incorporate demographic interventions and preference data that reflect the judgments of the raters and the institutions that employ them. The gap between the stated liberal commitment to open inquiry and the operational practice of selective prohibition is not subtle. A laboratory can publish papers celebrating scientific freedom while training its models to decline or soften answers on topics its safety team has marked as sensitive. The models acquire the boundary. They do not acquire an abstract commitment to Millian discussion that overrides the boundary.

The strengths remain real. Liberal jurisdictions have produced the majority of the frontier technical results since 2012. Open publication, labor mobility, and the residual capacity to criticize laboratory and government decisions have so far prevented the complete capture of the research agenda by any single authority. These are advantages relative to the alternatives, not guarantees. They can be eroded further by the same institutions that currently benefit from them. A laboratory under strong competitive or political pressure can narrow its refusal categories or expand them according to the incentives of the moment. Nothing in the technical architecture restores a constraint the trainers have stopped rewarding.

The predicted optimization targets under a continued liberal order are therefore mixed. Formal rights, scientific output, and the possibility of exit and criticism remain available as long as the institutions that enforce them continue to do so. Prohibited action classes will track whatever the laboratories and their regulators choose to penalize. Those choices have already diverged from the older texts on questions of speech, association, and equal formal treatment. The divergence can widen. It can also be reversed. The technical systems will amplify whichever version of the constraint set is in force when recurrent self improvement becomes difficult to oversee. They will not spontaneously recover a version the trainers have abandoned.
